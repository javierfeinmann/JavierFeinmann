Higher Education around the World · release v2.2 · October 2026

Documentation

Who controls the most internationally prominent universities of each country, and what does a domestic student pay to attend them? This dataset codes the top universities of 63 countries and territories — 376 institutions, selected by three rankings (below) — for control (public or private), the standard annual charge to a domestic first-degree student relative to GDP per capita, and the existence of means-tested support, by who funds it and how it is paid. Every institution is backed by documented sources (3,948 in total), each with its URL, access date and a verbatim quote.

How to cite. If you use these data, please cite:

Feinmann, Javier and Roberto Hsu Rocha. “Inequality and Higher Education: The Role of Elite Public Colleges.” Working paper. [PDF]

and, where useful, the release: Higher Education around the World dataset, v2.2 (October 2026).

Corrections, missing sources and suggestions are very welcome: jfeinmann@berkeley.edu.

1. Sample

Three samples, each fixed before any price of its new institutions was collected. The two QS 2027 samples cover 63 countries and territories; the QS 2024 sample covers the 55 of the earlier releases. In every sample, all institutions tied at the fifth position are included and countries with fewer than five ranked institutions keep all they have.

  • QS 2027, overall rank (the default in the visualisation): the five highest-ranked institutions per country in the QS World University Rankings 2027 (published June 2026).
  • QS 2027, academic reputation: the five institutions per country with the highest QS Academic Reputation score, from QS's survey of academics. This is arguably closer to what "elite" means, because the overall rank also rewards indicators such as the faculty/student ratio on which small institutions score high.
  • QS 2024, overall rank: the sample of the first release (QS 2024, published 27 June 2023), kept as a robustness check.

The two 2027 samples share 268 institutions; the overall-rank sample shares 248 with QS 2024 and the reputation sample 225. The dataset holds the union of the three. Aid and enrolment refer to 2023/24; prices are given both for 2023/24 and for the latest year (section 2). The 2027 samples use a recent ranking to choose the institutions, not to date the prices. Which institutions are "top 5" can change a lot across editions: in Uruguay, for example, the Universidad de Montevideo was first in QS 2024 and the Universidad de la República first in QS 2027.

2. What is measured

Reference student and year. A citizen, locally resident, full-time student in a first undergraduate degree, at a standard load, in academic year 2023/24 (or calendar 2023). Where a source for that year could not be found and the same regime clearly applied, the nearest year is used and flagged (36 institutions carry a year label other than 2023/24 or calendar 2023; see year_observed).

Control (strict). An institution is public if the state owns it (a public-law body created by the state, with no private founder) or appoints a majority of its governing body; otherwise it is private (non-profit or for-profit). Public funding and academic autonomy do not enter this definition. Separately, a government-dependence flag marks private institutions that receive substantial public funds explicitly for tuition or teaching (for example per-student teaching grants or the state paying students' fees). Research funding and student loans do not count.

Headline charge. Standard annual tuition plus compulsory recurring fees paid by every reference student, after reductions that apply to all students in every year of study, and before any targeted aid. Housing, health insurance, application and entrance-exam fees are excluded. Where prices vary by programme, the headline is the midpoint of the published range (minimum and maximum are kept); where they depend on family income, it is the full-pay rate. Reductions limited to the first year are not part of the headline.

Price bands. The headline charge divided by GDP per capita 2023 in current local currency (World Bank; for Taiwan, the official national accounts), grouped in bands fixed before coding: free < 1%, low 1–5%, low–moderate 5–10%, moderate 10–25%, high ≥ 25%. An alternative set of cut-offs (2.5 / 5 / 15 / 30%) is reported as a sensitivity check.

Two price years. Every institution has a 2023/24 price (columns without prefix) and a latest price (latest_* columns), collected in September–October 2026 with the same rules for academic year 2025/26 or calendar 2025. Each price is divided by GDP per capita of its own year (World Bank, July 2026 release; Taiwan from its national accounts). The latest view has a band for 367 institutions: 343 with a 2025/26 price and 24 that use an earlier year’s price with that year’s GDP per capita (no 2025/26 price could be documented, or no GDP or exchange rate exists for its year; marked * and explained in latest_year_flag). 13 institutions are in a different band in the two views. Control, aid and enrolment are not recoded in the latest view. Its 1,265 sources are in price_sources.csv; the independent checks of the rows that changed band or rules, corrected a 2023/24 figure, or belong to the countries added in 2026 (80 rows) are in price_verification.csv.

Means-tested support. Non-repayable support whose eligibility depends on household or parental income, coded in four cells: funded by the government or run by the institution, times paid as a fee reduction (waiver, income-scaled fee, or grant paid against the charge) or as cash to the student (for example living-cost grants). Each cell is yes, no (searched, not found), unknown, or not_applicable (a fee reduction where the charge is zero). Loans, in-kind support and merit-only awards are excluded. Aid is never subtracted from the headline charge. Where documented, the share of students receiving each instrument is recorded.

QS classification (context only). QS publishes, for each ranked institution, a Status (public / private not for profit / private for profit), a size class, a subject focus and a research intensity; they are released as context variables (qs_*) and are not used in any coding. QS Status is not our control variable: QS "Public" also covers private universities that depend on public funding. Of the institutions classified as private under the strict test, 18 are "Public" in QS (for example Oxford, Cambridge, UCL, Imperial, the Israeli universities and Hungary's foundation universities); 5 institutions public under the strict test are "Private" in QS.

Enrolment. One figure per institution for 2023/24 or 2023: first-degree headcount where published (242 institutions), otherwise undergraduate headcount, total headcount or full-time equivalents (flagged; 114 institutions). 3. Country summaries

3×3 grid. Institutions are placed by control (public; private and government-dependent; private and not government-dependent) and price (free; low to low–moderate; moderate or high). A country is placed in the cell that holds the largest number of its priced institutions (unweighted) or the largest share of their enrolment (enrolment-weighted); a country tied between two cells counts half in each. Institutions without an established price are left out.

Country type (rule fixed in advance). Control type from the share of public institutions: public if at least 0.7 (4 or 5 of 5), mixed if 0.3 to 0.7, private below 0.3. Price type is the median band; it is marked incomplete when a missing price could move the median. Each country type is recomputed under several alternative definitions (top 1 and top 3 only; alternative band cut-offs; government-dependent private counted as public; enrolment weights; the low and high ends of programme ranges; pricing the free track where free places are rationed by exam score; setting the price to zero where a documented majority of entrants pay nothing). These are the sens_* columns of countries.csv.

Weights. Summary shares are shown pooled (each institution counts once), per country (country shares averaged with equal weight) and per country, enrolment-weighted. The association with income inequality uses the World Bank Poverty and Inequality Platform Gini (latest national survey 2013–2023) and is descriptive only.

4. How the data were collected and checked

Collection. The data were collected with the help of AI research agents (Anthropic's Claude) working under a written protocol set by the investigators. Collectors were given the definitions and the distinctions to keep separate, and were never told what result to expect for a country. Every fact had to be supported by an opened source: the exact URL, a verbatim quote in the original language, a locator and the access date. Where a live page had changed since 2023/24, contemporaneous archived captures (Internet Archive) were used. Unknown is recorded as unknown: a missing price or a missing grant is never read as zero.

Independent check. Each institution was checked once by a second agent working in a fresh context, which re-opened the cited sources (and others where needed) and asked whether they give the same control and the same price band. Exact figures need not match; categories must. Control agreed for 373 of 376 institutions and the band for 361; the exceptions are listed in section 6. Disagreements were resolved with evidence or left unresolved, and every correction was applied by script, so all tables can be rebuilt from the recorded evidence. The checker is the same kind of model as the collector: a fresh context reduces anchoring, but errors need not be independent.

Human review. The investigators set the protocol and made every methodological decision (section 5), reviewed contested cases and commissioned a skeptical review of the country-level conclusions. Not every individual value has been re-verified by hand. That is one reason every source is published: anyone can follow a coding back to the page it rests on.

6. Admissions: selectivity and formal equity

For the 308 institutions of the QS 2027 academic-reputation sample, the release adds how first-year, first-degree entry worked for 2023/24 entry (or the nearest year, flagged): applicants, admitted, entrants and places, the admission regime, and whether the institution applied a formal equity admission rule.

Formal equity admission (equity_formal) means an explicit rule that reserves places, adds points, lowers the admission threshold or opens a separate entry track for applicants from groups defined by disadvantage strongly correlated with income: household income or socioeconomic status, race or ethnicity (including Indigenous peoples), caste or tribe, public or under-represented schools, rural or remote origin and disadvantaged regions, first generation, care leavers and orphans. It is not: holistic “considered” factors; individual hardship clauses; channels that apply the same criteria (QuestBridge); quotas defined by territory, religion or language (e.g. Greenland, Azores and Madeira, the Thrace Muslim minority, the Sámi language quota, occupied or earthquake regions) or by gender; merit, athlete, legacy, veteran, international or service-obligation routes; and financial aid. Rules for applicants with disabilities are recorded separately (equity_disability_rule) and do not make equity_formal yes (decisions D066–D074). equity_scope says whether the rule covers first-degree entry across the institution, only named programmes, or only an access or foundation programme. Reserved and filled counts are given with their own denominators; a share is reported only as stated by the source or computed from stated counts.

Rates. Admission rate (admitted / applicants) and entry rate (entrants / applicants) are released only where the independent check found the figures on the same base (population, year and round): 63 institutions. Applicant counts are often applications (one person counted once per programme) rather than persons (sel_unit), and under central allocation (e.g. Greece, Turkey, China) institutions have no applicant count at all.

Collection and check. One collector run per country, then an independent checker per group of countries that coded equity blind before seeing the draft and re-opened the main source of every figure: equity agreed for 289 of 308 institutions before reconciliation; 188 corrections were accepted by the coordinator (admissions_verification.csv, sources in admissions_sources.csv).

7. Limitations and known issues

  • Which universities. The sample is defined by an international research ranking. It says nothing directly about the rest of each system (non-ranked public universities, vocational and short-cycle institutions), where most students often study. Five institutions per country is a small window.
  • Which countries. 63 countries and territories chosen by the investigators, not a random sample. Countries with fewer than five ranked institutions, or more because of ties, are listed per sample in section 1.
  • Which ranking. The top 5 depends on the ranking edition and criterion (section 1). The three samples are reported side by side so that results can be compared across definitions; none of them is the universe of selective institutions.
  • Sticker price, not net price. The headline charge is what a student pays before targeted aid. For low-income students at institutions with generous means-tested aid (for example elite US private universities) the net price can be far lower. Aid is coded as present or absent, not by generosity; coverage shares are recorded only where documented. 569 of the 376×4 aid cells are unknown, so shares of institutions with aid are lower bounds.
  • One reference student. Charges for international students, part-time students, second degrees and separately priced tracks are not covered.
  • Programme variation. Where prices vary by programme, the midpoint can sit in a different band from the cheapest or most expensive programmes (band_unstable, and the low-end / high-end sensitivities).
  • Denominator. GDP per capita in current local currency. For Ireland it is inflated by multinational accounting, which lowers Irish ratios; purchasing-power comparisons across countries are not the aim.
  • Close to a cut-off. 50 institutions sit within 10% (relative) of a band threshold, so a small change in price or GDP would move them.

  • Contested control. Some classifications turn on legal details and are reported with sensitivities: English universities are private non-profit and not flagged as government-dependent (borderline); Edinburgh and McGill are private non-profit and flagged; the Israeli universities and Hungary's foundation universities are private and flagged; Antwerp's control decides whether Belgium is mixed or private. Board composition was not documented systematically, so a stricter test requiring a state-appointed board majority is not reported.
  • Latest prices. Some latest prices rest on weaker evidence than the 2023/24 rows (press reports, a university's statement that fees were unchanged, or estimates), flagged with low confidence in latest_confidence. Prices in high-inflation economies (Argentina, Turkey, Egypt) depend strongly on the month observed; the price and GDP years are matched, but not the months.
  • Gaps from the 2027 update. The institutions added from QS 2027 were collected and checked in September 2026. Some have no established price (a fee schedule was not published or not reachable; see the list above), and a few prices rest on weaker evidence (a nearest year, a news report or field averages), flagged with low confidence in confidence and explained in notes. Part of the checking of these additions was done without web search: only the cited and directly reachable sources were re-opened.
  • Open items in this release. Whether Saudi Arabia's Prince Mohammad bin Fahd University should be flagged as government-dependent (the share of its entrants paid by the Ministry is unknown; currently not flagged). POSTECH is coded free under the rule for tuition waived unless a student falls behind; this application is under review. Khalifa University (UAE) is coded free for nationals, but its scholarship tiers may leave some Emiratis paying (AED 82,500 a year); Baku State University is high on its main campus and moderate if its Qazax branch is counted; who appoints the University of Nizwa's board is not documented (counted in the “unresolved as public” sensitivity). Four of the six Uzbek prices rest on the national base contract schedule, which the universities were required to keep in 2023/24, not on their own schedules.
  • Enrolment. Measures differ across institutions (first-degree, undergraduate, total headcount or FTE), and some counts are course enrolments rather than students. Enrolment is missing for 376−356 institutions; a country with any such institution in a sample is left out of that sample's weighted results rather than imputed.
  • Inequality. The Gini comparison is an association across a small number of countries with strong regional clustering, not an effect. Consumption-based Ginis (China, Egypt, India, Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, Nigeria, Pakistan, Philippines, South Africa, Thailand, Ukraine) are lower than income-based ones; Azerbaijan, Hong Kong, New Zealand, Oman, Saudi Arabia, Singapore have no survey on the platform; Argentina has only an urban survey.
  • Admissions variables. Many institutions publish no applicant or offer counts; figures come from different sources (institutional reports, national statistics, admission systems) and scopes, documented in sel_scope and adm_notes. Equity rules were read for 2023/24 entry where an archived or dated text was available; some rest on the nearest year or on secondary sources (flagged in notes). Five institutions remain unknown because their sources were blocked or not found. Finance (revenue by source) was piloted but is not released.
  • Quotes. Source quotes longer than 400 characters are shortened in this release (1067 quotes, marked in quote_truncated); the URLs lead to the full text.

10. Versions and updates

The dataset will be extended with more institutions per country and more countries. Each release gets a new version number, is generated by code from the recorded evidence, and is never edited by hand; the changes are listed here.

  • v2.2 (October 2026): admissions variables for the 308 institutions of the QS 2027 academic-reputation sample: applicants, admitted, entrants, places, admission regime, formal equity admission rules (with scope, basis, instrument, mandate, reserved and filled counts) and disability rules, each collected and independently checked (admissions_sources.csv, admissions_verification.csv); a table of which types of institutions have formal equity rules (equity_by_type.csv, equity_by_country.csv, section 6). Prices and classifications unchanged from v2.1.
  • v2.1 (October 2026): latest prices (academic year 2025/26 or calendar 2025) for every institution next to the 2023/24 prices, each divided by GDP per capita of its own year (latest_* columns, price_sources.csv, price_verification.csv, a price-year switch in the visualisation); eight countries added (Slovakia, United Arab Emirates, Oman, Philippines, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Azerbaijan, Kyrgyzstan); 376 institutions in 63 countries and territories. Corrections of 2023/24 figures: Universidad de Lima (an enrolment fee of S/ 250 per semester had been missed), Universidad de Granada (a file-opening fee of €59.10, not €26.30), NTU “KhPI” (the range maximum was an English-taught programme), the University of Haifa (a range across programmes), PSL (range minimum); no band changed.
  • v2.0 (September 2026): samples from the QS World University Rankings 2027 (overall rank and academic reputation) added as headline samples, QS 2024 kept as a robustness check; 328 institutions in 55 countries and territories (54 new); QS classification variables added; prices still 2023/24. Correction: a build-order error had let an earlier recode overwrite a later ruling, so the Free University of Berlin and RWTH Aachen showed institution-funded fee aid; both are now no (the fund is run by the student body and tested on the student's own income).
  • v1.0 (September 2026): first public release. 274 institutions in 55 countries and territories (QS 2024 top 5); protocol v1.4.
